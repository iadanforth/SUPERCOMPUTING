My fascination with biological diversity has been a consistent theme throughout my life.  I was raised by two entomologists, who nurtured my early interest in marine organisms by allowing me to become scuba certified at 12 years old. Family vacations became centered around scuba diving expeditions in pristine ecosystems including atolls in Belize and the Galapagos. After a marine ecology course at Shoals Marine Lab, I became captivated by the adaptations of intertidal organisms and sought out a laboratory in my hometown of Ithaca, NY that pursued related research. This led me to the Hewson Lab at Cornell University, where I described the biogeography of a sea-star associated densovirus. There, I learned that viruses are key drivers of marine diversity, constituting an integral and vastly understudied component of aquatic communities. The realization that such miniscule biological entities were enhancing the primary productivity supporting marine food chains, driving host evolution, and indirectly restructuring ecosystems through impacts on host mortality drove me to focus my career on understanding the ecology and evolution of marine viruses. 

As I have progressed in my research career, it has become evident that I require sophisticated computational skills to address the research questions that have followed me since high school. Most viruses, particularly those infecting eukaryotic phytoplankton, are unculturable. This requires researchers to rely on metagenomics to characterize ocean viromes, resulting in large quantities of data that must be analyzed through complex software programs to generate interpretable results. Inferring viral phylogenies, putative host associations, and impacts on host populations similarly relies on vast databases of viral sequences from environmental samples. Viral evolution is exceptionally difficult to resolve given their complicated biology, the absence of universal marker genes, and the lack of a single common ancestor. Many of the programs required for such analyses must be run through a high-performance computer, prompting my interest in Supercomputing for Science. 

My objective for this course is to gain experience with big-data, the command-line, and bioinformatics pipelines to feel comfortable with and capable of exploring the many new programs that are developed each year for viral metagenome assembly and evolutionary inference. I hope that this class will provide me with the skills to confidently navigate the W&M HPLC, which I will be using for my PhD project. As a Masters student, I received no training in project management and designed my own methods to document the progress of my data collection, sample processing, and statistical analyses. However, I recognize that my system is far from ideal, reducing the reproducibility of my work. For instance, I did not begin using GitHub to store the R Markdown document containing every model used in my Masters project until 4 months before graduating. Prior to this, I relied on carefully annotating the document with timestamps for important changes and creating new versions of the same document when significant modifications were made. This resulted in many instances where I could not remember which file was the most recent version or forgot that I had already implemented a change in the script at an earlier date. As a graduate student, time is a precious commodity, and I hope that this class will help me become more efficient as a PhD student. To ensure that I achieve these goals, I intend to take notes in class using R Markdown to write scripts with additional notes in plain text format. These notes will then be reviewed immediately after each class to generate a synthesis document of the topics covered and key information. To make my experience translatable to my research, I will try to use metagenomic data collected by a collaborator when possible so that I can ask relevant questions. In addition, all lecture and reading files will be stored on my Notability, where I can easily annotate the document and record questions as they appear. 

My current methods of documentation and script organization are difficult for anyone but myself to follow. As was discussed in the reading tied to this assignment, this renders my work difficult to reproduce and therefore limits its value. When a new graduate student begins to build on my project, I must review my work, or we ultimately publish this research, it is imperative that all analyses be easily reproducible. Importantly, exploring viral diversity in Chesapeake Bay eukaryotic algae is a novel research direction for VIMS, and one that I hope will continue long after I have graduated. With this in mind, I am making a concerted effort to thoroughly document the development of my protocols, ordering supplies, and miscellaneous notes, but am less familiar with how to properly organize aspects of the project related to metagenomic data. Consequently, I created folders within “assignment_01” for “config” (for settings used in whichever program I use), “docs” (for notes, daily records of issues, and protocols), “logs” (to record events occurring as a program runs), “data” (“clean” for my curated data removing extraneous information automatically generated by the program, and “raw” for the unaltered data), “results” (for the key results and my interpretation of them), and “scripts” (containing all scripts used to analyze my data). This structure will enhance the reproducibility of my work and the ease with which a new student or incoming PI can build off of my work by maintaining the sanctity of raw data, documenting edits to workflows via scripts, ensuring that the same outputs can be regenerated from these scripts, and using a README file as religiously as I use my lab notebook for my molecular work and experiments. In addition to an intuitive structure, including scripts that automate tasks to reduce the probability of simple errors, including checks in my scripts, and clearly annotating any documents will further render my work more reproducible. 

I am excited to finally acquire the skills needed to understand the beautifully labyrinthine viral world that I adore, and grateful for the opportunity to participate in this course.
